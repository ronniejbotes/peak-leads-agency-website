Lead quality

Exclusive vs shared leads: the real difference

Bradley Hart, Peak Leads · July 30, 2026

A shared lead is sold to 3-5 contractors at $15-60 each, so you are racing four other companies to the same homeowner. An exclusive lead goes to you alone and costs $41-150. Exclusive usually wins on cost per booked job, but only if you call fast and follow up.

Every lead vendor says quality. The word that actually matters is exclusive, and plenty of vendors hope you never ask about it. This post explains the difference with numbers, including the cases where cheap shared leads genuinely make sense. Full source-by-source pricing is in how much do roofing leads cost.

What is a shared lead?

A homeowner fills in one form on a marketplace like Angi, HomeAdvisor or Networx. The marketplace sells that form to 3-5 contractors at $15-60 each. One request for a quote becomes $75-300 of revenue for the platform. That is the business model, and it explains everything else about how shared leads behave.

From the homeowner's side: they asked once and now five companies are calling. Most pick whoever calls first, talk to two, and stop answering the phone. From your side: you paid for a race you may have already lost by the time the lead hits your inbox.

What is an exclusive lead?

An exclusive lead is sold to one buyer. You get the homeowner's details and nobody else does. Prices run $41-150 depending on trade, market and job type, roughly two to three times the shared price.

Read the fine print, because exclusive has flavors. Some vendors sell exclusivity for 24 or 48 hours, then recycle the lead. Some sell it exclusively per trade, so you share the homeowner with a plumber and a landscaper but not another roofer. Some sell old leads as fresh. Real exclusivity means the lead was generated recently, for you, and is never resold. Anything less should be priced closer to shared.

Which one costs less per booked job?

Here is the math with illustrative round numbers. Spend $1,200 either way.

  • Shared: 30 leads at $40. You reach 12 before the homeowner stops answering, quote 8, book 3. That is $400 per booked job, before the discount you gave to beat four other bids.
  • Exclusive: 12 leads at $100. You reach 9, quote 7, book 4. That is $300 per booked job, at full margin.

Swap in your own rates. The pattern holds: the sticker price says shared wins, the booked-job price usually says exclusive wins. Usually, not always, which brings us to the honest part.

What do shared leads cost beyond the price?

The sticker price is not the whole bill. Budget for three hidden costs before you commit.

Dialing time. Reaching a shared lead takes multiple attempts across several days, because the homeowner's phone is already busy with your competitors. Someone has to do that work, and their hours are a real cost that never shows up in the vendor's dashboard.

Credit disputes. Wrong numbers, homeowners outside your area, people who insist they never filled in a form. Marketplaces have credit policies for bad leads, but claiming those credits is its own part-time job, and the burden of proof sits with you.

Quote compression. Even when you win a shared lead, you won it against four other bids. The discount you gave to get the signature comes straight out of margin. Exclusive leads do not remove negotiation, but they remove the auction.

Does exclusive automatically mean good?

No, and this is worth being blunt about. Exclusivity tells you who else has the lead. It tells you nothing about whether the lead was worth having.

An exclusive lead can still be a wrong number, a renter with no authority to buy, or a homeowner three towns outside your service area. You are paying $41-150 for sole access, so the verification behind the lead matters more, not less. Before you buy, ask what gets checked before a lead is sent: is the phone number confirmed, is the address inside your territory, did the homeowner state an actual job? A vendor doing that work can defend the price. A vendor selling raw, unchecked form fills at exclusive prices is charging you twice for one promise.

When do shared leads make sense?

Shared leads are not a con. They are a volume game, and some contractors are built to win it.

  • You have someone whose actual job is calling new leads within five minutes, during business hours and after.
  • You need to fill crew capacity this week and cheap volume beats perfect economics.
  • You are testing a new territory and want signal without committing real budget.
  • You run a repair-focused business where job values are small and speed matters more than margin.

If none of that describes you, the cheap lead is the expensive one. Most owner-operators lose the race to firms with full-time dialers, then conclude that leads do not work. The leads were fine. The race was rigged against a busy owner on a roof.

How do you check if a vendor is really exclusive?

Five questions, in writing:

  • Who else receives this lead, now or at any point later?
  • How was it generated, and under whose brand did the homeowner enquire?
  • How old is the lead when it reaches me, and is delivery real time?
  • What is the replacement policy for wrong numbers and dead leads?
  • Can I see a sample lead with timestamps before I buy?

A straight vendor answers all five without flinching. A vague answer on the first question tells you the price should be a shared price.

How do you make any lead worth more?

Three things move the numbers more than the source does.

Speed. Call within minutes, not hours. This is the single biggest lever on contact rate for either lead type.

Follow-up. One call is not follow-up. A short sequence of calls and texts over the first week recovers leads that a single attempt writes off.

What they find when they search you. After your call, homeowners search your company name. A credible website, reviews and a visible local search presence close deals you never see happening. If your site looks abandoned, the exclusive lead quietly becomes nobody's lead.
