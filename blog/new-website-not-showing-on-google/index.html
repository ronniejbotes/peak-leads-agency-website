Getting found

Will my new website show up on Google?

27 August 2026

A new website usually shows up on Google for your business name, as long as nothing on the site tells Google to stay away. Google has to find the site through links or a sitemap, crawl it and decide to index it, and it does not guarantee any of those steps. Turning up when someone searches "electrician Centurion" is a separate job: it takes a page built for that search, a Google Business Profile and ongoing SEO against businesses that are already there.

Owners ask this before their new website is even built, and the answer comes in two halves. Showing up when someone types your business name is mostly technical: Google has to be able to find and read your pages. Showing up when someone types your trade and their suburb is a contest, and a new website enters it with nothing behind it.

This post deals with the first half: how Google finds a new site, how to check yours, and what to do when it is missing. The second half comes near the end. What you will not get is a date, because Google does not promise one either.

How does a new website get into Google?

Google has to find the site and store its pages in its index before it can show them to anyone. Its in-depth guide to how Search works describes three stages (crawling, indexing and serving results) and says plainly that not every page makes it through each one.

Finding comes first. There is no central register of web pages. Google discovers new addresses through links from pages it already knows, and through sitemaps: files listing a site's pages, which you can hand to Google directly. Crawling means Googlebot, Google's software, fetches the page and reads it much as your browser would. Indexing means Google works out what the page is about and stores it.

Two more lines in that guide matter before you pay anyone. Google does not accept payment to crawl a site more often or rank it higher. And it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or show any page, even one that follows its published guidelines. Anyone promising you a position or a date is promising something Google itself will not.

How do you check whether Google has your site?

Run a site: search on your cellphone, then confirm in Google Search Console. Both are free, and the first takes seconds.

Type site: and your domain into Google with no space, for example site:yourbusiness.co.za. If pages come back, Google has indexed those. If nothing comes back, it probably has not indexed the site yet. The list is not always complete, Google warns, so one missing page proves nothing.

While the phone is out, open your site in a private or incognito tab. Google's own checklist for missed sites suggests this, because a login page is one of the most common reasons it cannot crawl one. If a stranger gets a password box or a "coming soon" screen, so does Google.

For the proper answer, use the URL Inspection tool in Search Console, Google's free service for site owners. It checks one address at a time. "URL is on Google" means the page is indexed and can appear, though Google does not promise it will. "URL is not on Google" means it cannot, and the details underneath say why. If Google has not seen a page yet, click Test live URL. Two lines there do most of the diagnosing: "Crawl allowed?" and "Indexing allowed?". A No on either points to a setting on your own site.

Why is my new website not showing on Google?

On a new site, the cause is often something left over from the build, or simply that Google has had no way to find it yet. Check these in order.

  • A noindex rule is still switched on. It tells search engines not to index a page, which is sensible while a site is half built. Google drops any page carrying it from its results, so if it was left on every page, the whole site stays out. Your website platform may have a simple setting for it. URL Inspection shows it as a No next to "Indexing allowed?".
  • Your robots.txt file is blocking Google. This small file at yourbusiness.co.za/robots.txt tells crawlers which addresses they may visit, and one rule in it can shut them out of the whole site. Look for a No next to "Crawl allowed?".
  • A password or a holding page is still up. The private-tab check will have shown you this one.
  • The site you approved is still on the developer's temporary address, while your own domain shows the old site or a parking page. Google indexes what is at your address, not what was on the developer's screen.
  • Nothing points to the site yet. Google lists a site that has only just launched and a site nobody links to among the usual reasons it misses one, and a brand new domain is both. With no sitemap either, Google has to stumble on you.

The first four are leftovers that someone with access can fix. The last one takes work.

How do you get your new website on Google?

Clear whatever is blocking it, then give Google every fair way to find it. You can do most of this yourself.

  1. Get Search Console access in your own name, as a verified owner, not only as someone your developer added. Several people can verify the same site. If your developer is the only verified owner, your access hangs on theirs, which is why who owns your website tells you to make your own account an owner.
  2. Submit your sitemap in Search Console's Sitemaps report. Most website platforms create one for you. Google calls it a hint, not a guarantee, but for a site nobody links to yet it is the best list you can hand over.
  3. Run your home page and each service page through URL Inspection and request indexing. Once per page is enough. There is a daily limit, and asking twice does not get a page crawled any faster.
  4. Put the address everywhere your business already appears, starting with the website field on your Google Business Profile, your Facebook page and your Instagram profile. Google's starter guide says promotion, online and offline, speeds up discovery by search engines as well as people, and it even mentions business cards. For a trade, add the bakkie.
  5. Earn a few real links: a supplier that lists its installers, a trade body you belong to, the local business chamber, an estate that keeps a list of approved contractors. Do not buy them. Google warns that paying for links could be treated as a breach of its spam policies.

How long does it take for a new website to show up on Google?

Nobody can give you a date, Google included. Its page about getting your website on Google says: "It can take a few weeks for Google to notice a new site, or any changes in your existing site."

On its page about asking Google to recrawl your URLs, Google adds: "Requesting a crawl does not guarantee that inclusion in search results will happen instantly or even at all."

So two things sit outside everyone's control: when Google gets to your site, and whether it indexes every page once it does. If Search Console reports a page as crawled but not indexed, Google has read it and left it out for now. Its help says there is no need to resubmit it. Look at the page instead. Google lists low-quality content among the common reasons a page is not indexed, so if your suburb pages are one paragraph with the town name swapped, start there.

Why does your name show up but not your trade and suburb?

Because one is a lookup and the other is a contest. Few pages anywhere are about your business by name, so once the site is indexed it usually comes up. The exception is a generic name that half the town also uses.

A search like "electrician Centurion" is different. Google weighs what is most relevant to that person, including where they are, and a local trade search usually brings up map results. Those come from Google Business Profiles, ranked mainly on relevance, distance and popularity, and Google says nobody can pay for a better local position. The profile and the site do different jobs, as Google Business Profile vs website explains.

For the ordinary listings, Google needs a page that matches the search, so you need a page for each service and each area you cover. How many pages a trade website needs goes through that. Then Google needs a reason to rank your page above businesses that have answered the search for years. Links from other sites are one, and a new site has few or none. Earning them is ongoing SEO.

If you need calls from that search sooner, Google Ads is the paid route. Ads are the only part of that results page Google sells.
