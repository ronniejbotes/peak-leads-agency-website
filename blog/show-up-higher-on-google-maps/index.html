How does a trade business show up higher on Google Maps?

25 November 2026

Google ranks Maps results mainly on relevance, distance and how well known a business is. You can't move your yard closer to every customer, and you can't pay Google for a better spot. What you can do is make your Business Profile match the jobs people search for: the right category, every service listed, an accurate service area, real photos, a name with nothing added to it, and a website that says the same things. Then earn the rest, through reviews and links from other sites.

Here's what each part means for a plumber, electrician, roofer or builder.

What Google actually weighs

Google spells out its main factors on its local ranking help page, and keeps the finer detail to itself.

Relevance is how well your profile matches what someone typed. Distance is how far you are from the person searching. Prominence is how well known you are, which Google says takes in how many websites link to you and how many reviews you have.

Distance is the part you can't change, and it explains a result that confuses owners. If a searcher doesn't share where they are, Google uses what it knows about their location. So when you search "plumber" from your own yard in Benoni and see yourself near the top, you're seeing what someone at your yard sees. A homeowner in Boksburg sees a different list. Before you decide you're ranking well, or badly, ask a customer or a friend in the suburbs you want work from to search and send you a screenshot.

The same page is just as blunt about shortcuts: there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. So be wary of anyone who promises you a guaranteed spot on the map.

Get the category right before anything else

Your primary category tells Google what kind of business you are, and Google says outright that the categories you select affect your local ranking.

Choose the most specific category Google's list offers. Google's own example is picking "Nail salon" rather than "Salon". For a trade, that might mean an electrician category rather than a general contractor category, if electrical work is what you want more of. You can't make up your own category, and Google asks for the fewest categories that describe your core business, so don't add a category for every job you'll take.

A warning before you change it: editing a category can mean Google asks you to verify the business again. Do it when you have time to deal with that.

List every service you want calls for

Categories say what you are. Services say what you do. Service businesses may be able to list their services on the profile, choosing from suggestions for their type of business or adding custom ones, and Google says a service may be highlighted on your profile when someone searches for it.

Go through the jobs you want more of and check each is there: geyser replacement, leak detection, DB board upgrades, roof waterproofing, whatever brings in your best work. Keep prices and phone numbers out of the service names, because Google rejects custom services that include them.

Set your service area honestly

If you go to customers rather than them coming to you, you're a service-area business. Google's guidelines say to hide a home address, with the example of a plumber working from home, and to set a service area made of towns, postal codes or other areas, not a radius around your house.

Be as specific and accurate as you can. List the suburbs you actually drive to. A service-area business gets a profile for the whole area it serves, so don't make a second profile for the next town, and don't rent a virtual office to get an address there. Google's guidelines say a virtual office isn't eligible.

Leave your name alone

This is the rule that can cost you the profile. It's tempting to add "Plumbing Geysers Benoni" to the end of the name on the bakkie. Google's guidelines say your name should be your real-world name, as it appears on your signage and stationery, and that unnecessary information in it isn't permitted and could get the profile suspended. Services and place names are on Google's list of things that don't belong there.

Google may suspend profiles that don't follow its guidelines, and getting a suspended profile back means an appeal. So the name stays as it is on your invoices, and the services and suburbs go in the fields built for them.

The same goes for the rest of the profile. The description is for what you do, with no links and no prices or specials. Keep your hours to the hours customers can actually reach you. If you genuinely work by appointment only, Google's guidelines list that among the cases that shouldn't show hours at all.

Make your website say the same things

Google doesn't only read your profile. It builds what it shows about a business partly from crawled web content, such as your own website. So the site should back up the profile: the same name, the same number, the same services and the same areas.

This is also where a website earns its place next to the profile. The profile is a listing; a site can have a page for each service and each area you work. We cover that split in Do you still need a website if you have a Google Business Profile?.

Earn the prominence part: reviews and links

You can't fill this in on a form. It comes from reviews and from other sites mentioning you.

On reviews, Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, and that positive reviews and helpful replies can help a business stand out. Ask every customer, not only the ones you think were happy. Never offer anything for a review. Reply to the ones that come in.

On links, think about who already knows your work: the supplier whose products you install, the body you're registered with, the estate or school you've done jobs for, the local paper that covered a storm clean-up. A listing or a mention with a link from any of them is the kind of thing Google says it takes into account.

Photos and posts help the customer decide

Photos only show after your profile is verified, and Google asks that they represent reality: in focus, well lit, without heavy filters. Real photos of real jobs (the new geyser in its drip tray, the finished roof, the labelled DB board) do more for a homeowner deciding who to phone than any stock picture.

Posts show on your profile too, and Google says they help customers decide. Google doesn't list posts on its ranking tips page, so post them for the customer reading your profile. And keep phone numbers out of the post text, because Google may reject a post whose number it can't connect to your business.

Check what people searched to find you

After your profile is verified, its Performance report shows the search terms people used to find your business. You can't choose those searches, but you can read them. If people find you for "plumber" and never for "leak detection", and leak detection is the work you want, that's a service to add, a photo to post and a page to build on your site.

If you notice a rival's profile with a stuffed name or a fake address, anyone can suggest an edit to a profile they believe is inaccurate. Fix your own first, though, because that's the part you control.

What to do this week

  • Ask someone in a suburb you want work from to search for your trade and send you a screenshot.
  • Check your primary category is the most specific match Google's list offers.
  • Add every service you want more of to the services list.
  • Make your service area the suburbs you actually drive to.
  • Take anything that isn't your real business name out of the name field.
  • Make sure your website shows the same name, number, services and areas as the profile.
